From virtual restaurants to wearable in-game NFTs, consumers are growing accustomed to seeing brand logos in so-called metaverse platforms and on Web3 applications that are based on blockchain technology.

But they are also realizing that the very characteristics that make the promises of Web3 appealing to users — anonymity and the lack of a central governing authority — can allow for widespread intellectual property and trademark infringements.

Some brands are proactively fighting back. At the beginning of February, Nike filed a civil lawsuit against StockX, alleging that the online reseller's virtual NFT sneakers infringed on its trademarks. Hermès filed a lawsuit against Mason Rothschild at the end of 2021, claiming that the artist's fuzzy NFT "MetaBirkins" infringed on its iconic Birkin handbag trademark. And Miramax sued director Quentin Tarantino in November last year, after he tried to auction NFTs of parts of his screenplay for "Pulp Fiction." Miramax claimed the studio holds the rights to sell NFTs related to the film. The defendants in all three cases have denied the allegations.

Insider spoke to four intellectual property lawyers and legal experts, who acknowledged that the early Wild West feel of the metaverse and Web3 applications can make them terrifying environments for some brands. They shed light on why brands need to consider Web3 characteristics like permanence, decentralization, and anonymity when protecting their intellectual property.

"If there are no takedown mechanisms in Web3 that allow you to enforce your IP, it could be possible that IP will effectively disappear in Web3," Danielle Garno, a partner at the law firm Reed Smith, said.

Transactions recorded with blockchain technology like NFTs are permanent. This poses a conundrum for brands. On one hand, they can't "move fast and break things," à la early-2010s Facebook, Garno said. Indeed, Web3 experts have advised that marketers need to get up to speed on Web3 ideologies first, to avoid coming across as money-grabbers by jumping in too quickly.

On the other hand, companies do need to move quickly to ensure their trademarks and copyrights are protected in Web3 environments before someone else stakes a permanent virtual claim to a brand or copyrightable work. As of now, the best way to do so is by registering trademarks and copyrights in the real world through institutions like the US Patent and Trademark Office and US Copyright Office, said Joel Schmidt, a lawyer at Cowan, Liebowitz & Latman. He highlighted that brands can take advantage of specific categories that cover things like NFTs.

There are further IP complications when it comes to Web3. In a typical infringement case, a judge may order the infringing goods to be physically disposed of or erased from the web.

But in Web3 environments, "in order to effectively take them out of circulation, you would have to 'burn' them, which renders them valueless," Garno said. Burning, in this instance, refers to sending the digital asset associated with an NFT to a null address where no one has access to it and it can't be sold or transferred.

Garno wrote in a recent blog post that when it comes to the Hermès versus Rothschild case, "it raises issues as to the third parties who purchased a MetaBirkin NFT and whether they will be allowed access to their NFT if a court rules against Rothschild."

In the early days of the web, decentralization was the defining ideology, allowing users to post anything on the web without having to obey a central authority. That becomes achievable by distributing computing processes across many nodes instead of a central server, as is the case with platforms like Google and Facebook.

This poses a problem for brands: Unlike on social-media platforms, it's unclear who's moderating the space.

"It's not like you can just send an email to Facebook, Twitter, or Amazon and say 'take this down,'" John Shaeffer, a partner specializing in IP law at Fox Rothschild, said. "But that has repercussions going the other way, that a lot of competitors will just call Amazon saying, 'Take this product down because it's ripping mine off,' and it's not true."

Anonymity and pseudonymity are two of the biggest draws for Web3 users. But there is some potential recourse for brands that want to pursue infringers in the metaverse, thanks to Web3's inherent traceability.

This is due to Web3's reliance on blockchain technology like Ethereum, where most NFTs are minted. According to its creators, "each token minted has a unique identifier" and "every NFT must have an owner and this is of public record and easy for anyone to verify."

Those identifiers are also known as public keys, though they cannot be directly traced to real-world identities, so it could prove tricky to identify the real-world person behind a specific event on the blockchain. Experts said that, much like online counterfeiting and infringement, enforcement strategies around identifying potential defendants are likely to evolve over time.

"With decentralization you have unique fingerprinting, unique content, unique IP that drives it right back to the initial creator," Kirstine Stewart, the chief revenue officer at Pex, an online copyright compliance firm, said.

Identification, while possible, might not be desirable as a brand-safety strategy in Web3 and metaverse applications.

"One consideration is, 'Do we want to be seen as a trademark bully?,'" Garno said. "The other one is, 'Who am I enforcing against? And will the PR backlash be worse?'"

Garno said brands should ask if there is a way to work with the alleged infringer while still enforcing their intellectual property rights, "instead of coming down super hard with scary legal terms."

"If I'm a fan of Spider-Man and I want to go all over the metaverse dressed as Spider-Man, does Marvel want to prosecute that?" Shaeffer said. "That's really not a legal issue. That's a business issue that no one's been able to balance very well yet."
