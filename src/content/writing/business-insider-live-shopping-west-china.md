TikTok is dropping out of the e-commerce race in the US and Europe after its liveshopping foray in the UK failed to gain traction, people with knowledge of the matter told the Financial Times earlier this week.

It is a stark reversal of fortunes for TikTok's parent ByteDance. It's a company that jostles with tech giants Alibaba and JD.com for ecommerce dominance in China, where influencers using its livestream shopping function routinely sell millions of dollars worth of goods in a day.

But for experts, TikTok pulling its liveshopping operations out of Western markets is no surprise.

China marketing experts told Insider in April that tech companies and brands in the West might be investing in the wrong "next big thing." They said cultural differences between the two regions might be too difficult to overcome and it will take a big leap for shoppers to lose their entrenched tech habits.

But that hasn't deterred major companies from investing heavily in an attempt to strike the social ecommerce gold, with an eye on the booming live-streaming shopping market in China.

Walmart has announced it will host regular live shopping streams on Talkshoplive, a live shopping platform. Meta, YouTube, and Amazon have been co-opting influencers to host live shopping streams on their platforms. Elsewhere, VCs have been pouring money into the space. Some small US businesses also found a lifeline in live shopping during COVID-19-induced lockdowns.

Yet despite all the recent activity, the live shopping market in the US "is so slim that we don't forecast it," according to Insider Intelligence. In comparison, the live shopping market in China was worth almost $300 billion as of the end of 2021, and is on track to be worth almost half a trillion dollars by the end of this year.

"It's not going to happen, certainly not on the same scale as in China," said Arnold Ma, founder of Qumin, a marketing agency helping brands like Marriott and Papa John's expand in China. "You see people selling literally billions worth of products in the same day. I'm going to stick my neck out and say that's never going to happen in the west, on live shopping."

While the premise of live shopping in the West isn't new — QVC was a TV-retail powerhouse for decades — there are key differences in the way Chinese livestreamers have tapped into local culture that has propelled the trend into the mainstream.

"Chinese Gen Z is beginning to look inward to appreciate and support traditional Chinese culture," said Sarah Yam, founder of Red Digital, a Chinese marketing consultancy.

This movement, named "guochao", which translates as "national trend," has exploded in recent years, according to Yam, and has helped transform almost-forgotten Chinese firms into cult brands.

"Guochao is a kind of modern pop, patriotism, or old-school childhood nostalgia. It encourages Chinese livestreamers to support and promote homegrown brands," she added.

Many livestreamers have gained followings in the millions for showcasing traditional aspects of Chinese life, such as farmer Liu Mama on short-video app Kuaishou and rural vlogger Li Ziqi on TikTok's Chinese sister app Douyin.

Nascent Chinese brands have a lot to gain from partnering with top livestreamers: makeup brand Florasis said its yearly sales quadrupled after partnering with livestreaming star Austin Li in 2020.

China marketing experts said Chinese consumers look to live shopping as a form of entertainment.

"Westerners are more likely than the Chinese to view shopping as a chore, something on a to-do list, whereas Chinese tend to view it more as a method of entertainment or leisure," said Malmsten, of Daxue Consulting.

Even top Chinese livestreamers who are overtly pushing products — like Austin Li, known as the "Lipstick King" and famous for his "买买买!" ("Buy buy buy!") catchphrase — are seen as entertainers, said Qumin's Ma. Li is known for his scathing product reviews and for selling 15,000 lipsticks in 5 minutes, beating Alibaba founder Jack Ma in a one-on-one selling competition.

"It's like reality TV," said Ma. Whereas "on TikTok, it's just a really cringe person saying, 'Which handbag do you wanna look at? This one, or this one?' They don't have any charisma. There's no personality. It lacks the entertainment factor."

A big part of live shopping's success in China hinges on two unique technological differences compared with the US: Chinese consumers use their phones more than their computers to shop online, and they benefit from a seamless online shopping experience thanks to super apps, which aggregate content, shopping, and more in one place.

"When the iPhone 3G first came out, in China, the internet penetration was 40%, maybe 50%. So most people's first experience of the internet was in a mobile environment, which meant that people didn't have the legacy behavior of, 'Oh, I'm not really used to the idea of shopping on my phone'," said Ma.

The evolution of social media in China also offers clues as to why live shopping took off in the country. While in the west, platforms like Facebook and Instagram launched with text and image-based content, in China the biggest social media platforms like Douyin and Kuaishou were video heavy from the outset.

"It was more natural as an evolution that people wanted to be closer to creators. They had video, so what's the next best thing? It's to be able to watch them live," said Ma.

But watching people livestreaming doesn't automatically translate into revenue — and that's where super apps come in, by providing an integrated and seamless one-stop-shop experience.

The current ecommerce model in the US has given the rise to behemoths like Amazon and Shopify. Platforms like Instagram and Snapchat are relative newbies when it comes to shopping integrations, having released their shopping products only in the last two years.

A US consumer might learn about a brand on Instagram, research it on Google, read reviews on Amazon, before finally purchasing it on the brand's website, said Allison Malmsten of Daxue Consulting, a consulting firm helping businesses navigate the Chinese market. Meanwhile, Chinese tech platforms are far more advanced at including all these steps in one place.

"For example, you can purchase goods now directly through social media platforms like Xiaohongshu," added Malmsten, referring to the popular Chinese platform. Its shopping integration is so seamless that viewers don't need to leave the video they are watching to purchase a product.

Unlike their Western counterparts, influencers with large followings in China, known as Key Opinion Leaders or KOLs, have used their influence to dictate the prices of the goods they promote, said Yam, of Red Digital. On Douyin alone, the KOLs in the top 5% of follower count attracted 525 million users — more than the population of the US and UK combined.

Big name KOLs are also often backed by venture capitalists, who provide business support in the form of supply chain management, marketing, and celebrity management, added Yam.

"Live shopping combines shopping with entertainment in real time, with generally the best prices across all commercial platforms offline and online," said Elisa Harca, of Red Ant, a marketing consultancy focused on China.

While still in their infancy, similar behaviors could crop up among influencers in the US. Venture capital firms have already begun backing creators based on their advertising earnings from platforms like YouTube. And direct-to-consumer upstarts with experience in supply chain management have begun taking on a bigger role in the creator industry.

In the past couple years, top influencers like MrBeast and Emma Chamberlain have launched their own food and coffee brands in partnership with companies like Virtual Dining Concepts and UTA Ventures, the investment arm of Hollywood talent-agency UTA.

"It's simply a question of whether the return-on-time-investment is there for US-based creators. That will determine whether they actually pursue live shopping streams," said Detert. "If they can find a way to monetize another platform, it's worth their time and attention."
